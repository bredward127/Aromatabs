Brief awakenings are a normal feature of a night's sleep. They cluster in the
second half, they become more frequent with age, and most of them are never
remembered.[^ohayon2004b] By the early hours your deep sleep is largely spent
and you are cycling through lighter stages, so the threshold for waking is
simply lower than it was at midnight.

So the waking itself is usually not the problem. What happens next is.

## The ten minutes that decide it

If you surface, register the time, and drift off again, the episode leaves
almost no trace. If you surface and start calculating — four hours and twenty
minutes until the alarm, and a meeting at nine — you have introduced arousal
into a state that was about to resolve on its own.

This is the core of the cognitive model of insomnia: worry about sleep raises
arousal, arousal makes sleep harder, and the resulting bad night supplies fresh
material to worry about.[^harvey2002] The loop is self-sustaining, and it does
not require anything to be wrong with your sleep to begin with.

## What tends to help

Take the clock out of view. Knowing the time provides no information you can
act on and reliably supplies the arithmetic that keeps you awake.

If you are still awake and getting frustrated after what feels like twenty
minutes or so, get up. Go somewhere else, keep the light low, do something
undemanding, and go back when you feel sleepy rather than when you think you
should. This is the stimulus-control component of cognitive behavioural therapy
for insomnia, and it exists to stop your bed becoming a place your body
associates with lying awake.

Do not try to make up the lost time by staying in bed longer in the morning. It
feels sensible and it works against you, because it drops your sleep pressure
for the following night.

## When it is something else

Regular night waking with a dry mouth or a headache, snoring, or a partner
reporting pauses in your breathing is worth raising with a doctor. So is waking
several times to urinate, waking with reflux or pain, and night waking that
began abruptly. Persistent difficulty sleeping at least three nights a week for
three months or more meets the usual threshold for chronic insomnia and is
treatable — the first-line treatment is CBT-I, not medication.
