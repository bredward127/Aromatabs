Most advice about sleep treats it as a quantity. Get eight hours. Get seven.
Get more than you are getting. The number is easy to talk about, which is
probably why everyone talks about it.

But sleep is not a tank that fills. It is a sequence. Across a night your brain
runs through a repeating set of states, each with its own electrical signature,
its own chemistry, and its own job. The sequence is not random and it is not
evenly distributed. Understanding its shape explains a great deal that hours
alone cannot: why you can sleep eight hours and wake up flattened, why the last
part of the night feels different from the first, and why a late bedtime and an
early alarm damage two different things.

## The states your brain moves through

Sleep divides into two broad kinds, and the difference between them is stark
enough that early researchers assumed they were looking at two separate
phenomena.

**Non-REM sleep** is the quieter kind. It is conventionally split into three
stages of increasing depth. N1 is the doorway — a light, brief state you pass
through on the way down, where you may not even be certain you were asleep. N2
is the bulk of adult sleep, roughly half of the night, and it is where two
distinctive electrical events appear: sleep spindles, which are short bursts of
fast activity, and K-complexes, which are large single waves. N3 is the deep
one, also called slow-wave sleep, named for the high, slow waves that dominate
the recording. In N3 you are hard to wake, and if someone succeeds you will be
confused for a minute or two.

**REM sleep** is the other kind, and it looks almost nothing like the first.
Brain activity rises to something close to waking. Your eyes move rapidly under
the lids. Most vivid dreaming happens here. And your skeletal muscles are
actively paralysed, which is a safety feature rather than a fault — it stops you
acting out what you are dreaming.

You cycle between these. The conventional figure is ninety minutes per cycle,
and it is worth being clear that this is an average rather than a rule. Cycles
run from roughly seventy to a hundred and twenty minutes, they vary within a
single night, and they vary between people. A sleep tracker that tells you that
you completed exactly five ninety-minute cycles is presenting a tidier picture
than the underlying biology supports.

## The shape of the night is asymmetric

Here is the part that matters most, and the part most sleep advice leaves out.

The cycles are not identical. Deep sleep is concentrated in the first half of
the night. Your first cycle or two carry most of the N3 you will get; by the
early hours there is often very little left. REM runs the other way. Early REM
periods are short, sometimes only a few minutes. As the night goes on they
lengthen, and the longest REM periods arrive in the last couple of hours before
you wake.[^ohayon2004]

Two practical consequences follow, and they are not the same consequence.

If you go to bed at your usual time but set an alarm three hours early, you get
most of your deep sleep and lose a disproportionate share of your REM. If you go
to bed three hours late but sleep until your usual waking time, you lose deep
sleep instead. Both are "less sleep". They are not the same deficit, and people
often report them differently — the truncated night as emotionally raw, the late
night as physically leaden.

This also explains a common and slightly deflating experience. You wake
naturally an hour before the alarm, feel fine, and decide to use the time. You
have not necessarily made a bad trade, but you have almost certainly given up a
long REM period, and you may notice it in the afternoon rather than the morning.

## What decides when you get sleepy

Two systems govern the timing, and they work independently. This is the
two-process model, first set out by Alexander Borbély in 1982 and still the
organising framework for the field forty years on.[^borbely1982][^borbely2016]

**Process S is sleep pressure.** From the moment you wake, a drive to sleep
begins accumulating. It builds roughly in proportion to how long you have been
awake, and it discharges during sleep — fastest during deep sleep, which is part
of why the first hours of a night are the most restorative per minute. Adenosine
is the best-known chemical marker of this pressure, and caffeine works by
blocking its receptors, which is why coffee does not remove tiredness so much as
hide it.

**Process C is the circadian rhythm.** This is a roughly twenty-four-hour clock,
run from a small region of the hypothalamus, that pushes alertness up and down
independently of how long you have been awake. It is why you can be exhausted at
four in the afternoon, perk up at nine in the evening with nothing changed, and
then feel the floor drop out at eleven.

Sleep begins easily when both point the same way: high pressure, and a clock
that has begun its nightly descent. It goes badly when they conflict. Jet lag is
that conflict. So is a night shift. So, in a milder and more chronic form, is
going to bed at eleven when your clock is set for one.

The clock is set mainly by light. It is more sensitive to it than most people
expect — under controlled conditions, quite ordinary indoor light levels in the
evening are enough to shift the timing of melatonin release and suppress its
amount.[^zeitzer2000] This is the mechanism behind most of what we cover in
[wind-down](/wind-down) and in [environment](/environment).

## So how many hours?

The consensus recommendation for adults is seven or more hours a night on a
regular basis, from a joint statement by the American Academy of Sleep Medicine
and the Sleep Research Society.[^watson2015] The National Sleep Foundation's
panel arrived at a similar range, seven to nine hours for adults, with the
caveat that some people fall outside it without harm.[^hirshkowitz2015]

Both of those are population recommendations, and it is worth saying plainly
what that means. They describe where the risk curves bend for large groups. They
do not tell you your number. Individual need varies, it changes with age, and
the honest position is that there is no reliable way to establish your personal
requirement from a single night or a single week.

What we can say is that the shape matters alongside the total. Seven
uninterrupted hours and seven hours broken into three pieces are not equivalent,
because fragmentation costs you the long, late REM periods and interrupts the
deep sleep early on. Regularity matters too: a consistent schedule keeps Process
C and Process S aligned, and much of the benefit people attribute to "getting
enough sleep" comes from getting it at the same time.

## What this changes

Very little, immediately, which is the honest answer. Knowing the architecture
does not make you sleep better tonight.

What it does is reframe the questions. "Am I getting enough sleep?" becomes
several better ones. Am I sleeping at a consistent time? Am I cutting the night
short at the end, where REM lives, or starting it late, where deep sleep lives?
Is my sleep continuous, or am I losing the long cycles to fragmentation?

Those are answerable, and unlike your personal hour requirement, they are
mostly under your control.

## What the stages appear to be for

This is where we have to be careful about how confidently anything is stated,
because function is much harder to establish than structure. We can record the
stages precisely. Working out what they accomplish means disrupting them and
watching what breaks, which is slower work and leaves more room for argument.

The clearest picture is around memory. Sleep after learning improves retention
of that material, and the effect is not simply the absence of interference from
being awake. Slow-wave sleep appears to be particularly involved in
consolidating facts and events, and there is a plausible and well-supported
account in which memories are reactivated during deep sleep and gradually
stabilised.[^rasch2013] REM has been linked more to procedural and emotional
memory, though that division is neater in summary than it is in the literature.

A second line of work concerns clearance. Fluid movement through brain tissue
increases substantially during sleep in mice, which would allow metabolic waste
to be cleared more efficiently than during waking.[^xie2013] This finding
attracted a great deal of coverage, some of it well ahead of the evidence. The
honest position is that the mechanism is real and interesting in animals, that
the human picture is much less settled, and that anyone telling you a night of
bad sleep leaves toxins in your brain is overstating what is known.

Growth hormone release is tied closely to the first slow-wave period, which is
one of the more reliable stage-specific findings, and part of why the early
hours of sleep are hard to make up later.

## What a sleep tracker can and cannot see

Consumer trackers estimate sleep from movement and heart rate, sometimes with
blood oxygen. They are not measuring brain activity, which is what sleep staging
is defined by. When compared directly against polysomnography — the clinical
standard, with electrodes on the scalp — modern devices do reasonably well at
distinguishing sleep from wake, and considerably less well at telling the stages
apart.[^chinoy2021]

That is not a reason to throw the device away. It is a reason to read it
correctly. Bedtime, wake time and total sleep are the numbers most likely to be
approximately right, and they are also the numbers most worth acting on. A
nightly deep-sleep percentage is an estimate built on an estimate, and treating
it as a score to improve is a good way to start sleeping worse while trying to
sleep better.

If the number is making you anxious, that anxiety is itself now part of the
problem. Some people sleep better simply by turning the staging display off.

## When to see a doctor

Architecture is a framework for understanding ordinary sleep. It is not a
diagnostic tool. Speak to a doctor if you snore heavily or have been told you
stop breathing in your sleep; if you fall asleep unintentionally during the day;
if you have had difficulty sleeping at least three nights a week for three
months or more; if you act out dreams physically; or if your sleep changed
abruptly without an obvious cause.
