Restrict sleep across consecutive nights and performance degrades
cumulatively — the deficit on night seven is worse than on night two, even
though each individual night was the same length.[^vandongen2003] In that sense
the debt is real, and it is not an analogy.

Two findings from the same work are less comfortable, and they are the reason
the metaphor breaks down.

## You stop noticing

In controlled restriction studies, people's subjective sleepiness ratings
plateau while their objective performance keeps declining. You adapt to feeling
tired. You do not adapt to being impaired. This is why "I'm fine on six hours"
is such a common claim and such a poor guide — the person saying it is usually
reporting how they feel, accurately, and how they feel has stopped tracking how
they are doing.

## Repayment is partial, not one-for-one

Recovery sleep is efficient: after restriction, sleep gets deeper and the
pressure discharges faster. But performance does not simply bounce back in
proportion to the hours returned, and in some studies deficits persisted after
several nights of extended recovery sleep.[^belenky2003]

So a weekend lie-in helps. It does not settle the account, and it comes with a
cost of its own — sleeping until eleven on Sunday pushes your clock later and
makes Sunday night harder, which is how a bad Monday gets built.

## What to do with this

Consistency beats compensation. If you have a choice between averaging seven
hours with wild variation and averaging six and a half with a stable schedule,
the evidence does not clearly favour the first.

If you are genuinely short across the week, add the recovery at the front of the
night rather than the back. Going to bed thirty minutes earlier for five nights
does more than one long Sunday morning, and it does not move your clock.

And do not try to bank sleep in advance of a hard week. Sleeping longer before
restriction blunts the damage slightly in some studies, but the effect is small,
and the practice mostly produces people who lie in bed awake trying to sleep.
