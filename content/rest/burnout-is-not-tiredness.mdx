Burnout is used loosely to mean very tired. The research definition is narrower
and more useful.

Christina Maslach's model, which most of the literature is built on, identifies
three components: **exhaustion**, **cynicism or depersonalisation** — a
distancing from the work and the people in it — and **a reduced sense of
professional efficacy**.[^maslach2016]

The second component is the one that distinguishes burnout from tiredness. A
tired person still cares. Someone burnt out has stopped, and the not-caring is
protective rather than callous.

## It is occupational, not medical

The WHO classifies burn-out in ICD-11 as an occupational phenomenon — a factor
influencing health status — and explicitly not as a medical
condition.[^who2019] The definition is tied to chronic workplace stress that
has not been successfully managed.

That framing matters, because it locates the cause. Maslach's work consistently
identifies mismatches between person and job — workload, control, reward,
community, fairness, values — rather than individual resilience deficits. The
practical implication is uncomfortable for the wellness industry: burnout is
mostly a property of the job, and interventions aimed only at the individual
have a poor record.

## Why rest does not fix it

A holiday relieves exhaustion temporarily. It does not touch cynicism or
efficacy, and it returns you to the conditions that produced them. The
characteristic experience — feeling fine by the end of the second week and
dreading the return — is the diagnostic clue rather than a failure of the
holiday.

## What actually helps

Changing something structural: workload, autonomy, the amount of the job that is
meaningless, or the job. That is easier to write than to do, and we are not
going to pretend otherwise.

Where structural change is not immediately available, the evidence points toward
protecting detachment ruthlessly, rebuilding some sense of control over how the
work is done, and treating the cynicism as information about the situation
rather than as a personal failing.

## When it is something else

Burnout and depression overlap substantially and are not the same thing.
Persistent low mood, loss of interest that extends beyond work, changes in
appetite or sleep, or any thought of self-harm are reasons to see a doctor
promptly. Burnout is not a diagnosis you should be making on yourself.
