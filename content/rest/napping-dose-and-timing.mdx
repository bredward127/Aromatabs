Napping is one of the few sleep topics where the practical advice is genuinely
specific, because the dose-response relationship has been measured directly.

## The short answer

Ten to twenty minutes, in the early afternoon. That length improves alertness
and mood with minimal grogginess afterwards, and it is short enough that you are
unlikely to descend into deep sleep.[^hayashi1999]

The reason the upper bound matters is sleep inertia. If you wake out of
slow-wave sleep you will be disoriented and worse than useless for anything from
fifteen minutes to an hour. A study directly comparing nap durations after a
restricted night found a five-minute nap did little, a ten-minute nap produced
immediate and sustained benefits, and longer naps produced more sleep inertia
before their benefits appeared.[^brooks2006]

So the twenty-to-sixty-minute range is the one to avoid. It is long enough to
reach deep sleep and too short to get you out the other side of it.

## The long option

If you have ninety minutes and a genuine sleep debt, a full cycle is a different
proposition. You are more likely to wake from a lighter stage, and you get the
consolidation benefits that come with a complete cycle — the well-known
demonstration that a nap containing both slow-wave and REM sleep restored
perceptual performance about as well as a full night.[^mednick2003]

This is a real effect and a poor daily habit. Ninety minutes in the afternoon
discharges a great deal of the sleep pressure you were relying on at eleven that
night.

## Timing

Early to mid afternoon. There is a genuine circadian dip in alertness in the
post-lunch period that is not caused by lunch, and it is the point at which a
nap is both easiest to fall into and least disruptive to the night.

After about three or four in the afternoon, the cost side of the ledger rises.
The later the nap, the more of your night-time sleep pressure it spends.

## Who benefits, and who does not

Benefits vary with age, habitual napping, and how sleep-deprived you are to
begin with.[^milner2009] Habitual nappers tend to get more out of naps and
suffer less inertia. People who nap rarely often find them unpleasant, which is
a reasonable reason not to.

The most important exception: **if you have insomnia, do not nap.** Sleep
pressure is the main thing working in your favour at bedtime, and daytime
napping spends it. Sleep restriction — deliberately limiting time in bed to
build pressure — is a core component of CBT-I, and naps work directly against
it.

## The honest framing

A nap does not create sleep. It moves it. That is a fair trade when you are
genuinely short and have a demanding afternoon in front of you. It is a bad
trade when it becomes the way you get through every day, because the thing it
borrows from is the night.

If you need a nap most days, the question worth asking is not how long to nap.
It is why the night is not doing its job — and persistent daytime sleepiness,
particularly if you doze off unintentionally, is something to raise with a
doctor rather than manage with an alarm.
