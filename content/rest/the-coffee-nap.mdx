Drink a coffee, immediately lie down for fifteen to twenty minutes, get up when
the alarm goes.

The logic is tidy. Caffeine takes roughly twenty to thirty minutes to reach peak
effect, so it arrives as you wake. Meanwhile the nap clears some adenosine — the
molecule caffeine competes with — so the caffeine has less to block when it
lands.

## The evidence

The best-known test was in a driving simulator. Sleepy drivers were given
caffeine, a short nap, both, or a placebo. The combination outperformed either
alone at reducing lane drift and subjective sleepiness.[^horne1996]

Several small studies since have pointed the same way. The literature is thin
and mostly focused on driving and shift work rather than on office afternoons,
so the effect is best regarded as reasonably supported in the context it was
tested in rather than established generally.

## The catch nobody mentions

Caffeine has a half-life of around five to six hours. A coffee at two in the
afternoon is still substantially present at ten at night, and caffeine
demonstrably disrupts sleep even taken six hours before bed — including in
people who did not notice any effect.[^drake2013b]

So a coffee nap at two o'clock is borrowing from tonight twice over: once for
the nap, once for the caffeine.

## Where we would use it

Genuinely: before a long drive when you are sleepy and stopping is not optional,
and on a night shift. Both are situations where the alternative is dangerous
and the cost to that night's sleep is beside the point.

For an ordinary flat afternoon it is a lot of machinery for a modest gain, and
the same twenty minutes spent outside in daylight has no evening cost at all.
