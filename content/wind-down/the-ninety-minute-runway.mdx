Most people treat sleep as something that starts when they get into bed. The
lights go off, and the expectation is that unconsciousness follows within a few
minutes.

For some people it does. For a great many others it does not, and the reason is
that falling asleep is not an event. It is a handover — a set of physiological
changes that begin well before you lie down and take time to complete. If you go
from a lit room and an open laptop straight to a dark bedroom, you are asking
for the handover to happen in the two minutes after your head hits the pillow.

There are three things that have to happen, and none of them is fast.

## One: your core temperature has to start falling

This is the least intuitive part and probably the most important.

Sleep onset is tied closely to thermoregulation. In the evening, blood vessels
in your hands and feet dilate, which moves heat from your core out to the skin
and lets your core temperature fall. That fall is not a side effect of falling
asleep — it precedes it, and the speed of the heat redistribution predicts how
quickly you get to sleep.

The clearest demonstration of this is a small study published in _Nature_ in
1999, which found that the degree of dilation in the hands and feet was the best
physiological predictor of how fast subjects fell asleep — better than melatonin
levels or subjective sleepiness.[^krauchi1999] The broader thermophysiological
account has held up since.[^krauchi2007]

This is also why a warm bath before bed works, and why the mechanism is the
opposite of what most people assume. You are not being warmed into drowsiness.
You are dilating your peripheral blood vessels so that when you get out, your
core sheds heat faster than it otherwise would. A meta-analysis of passive body
heating before bed found modest but consistent improvements in how quickly
people fell asleep, with the effect strongest when the bath or shower was taken
roughly one to two hours before bedtime.[^haghayegh2019]

One to two hours. Not ten minutes. That timing is most of the reason the runway
has to be as long as it is.

The practical version, if you do not want a bath every night: warm hands and
feet, cool room, and stop doing anything that raises your core temperature —
including hard exercise — in the last hour or so.

## Two: your arousal has to come down

Arousal here means physiological and cognitive activation, not anything else.
Heart rate, muscle tension, and — the one that keeps most people awake — the
rate at which your mind is producing content.

Allison Harvey's cognitive model of insomnia describes the loop clearly: arousal
in bed produces monitoring, monitoring produces worry, worry produces more
arousal.[^harvey2002b] The unhelpful part is that the loop runs on the same
machinery whether the original content was a work problem or a worry about not
sleeping. Once you are lying in the dark with nothing else to attend to, your
attention will find something, and at eleven at night it is rarely cheerful.

What helps is giving the thinking somewhere to go before you are horizontal.

The most robustly demonstrated version of this is unglamorous: write tomorrow
down. In a polysomnography study, participants who spent five minutes writing a
specific to-do list before bed fell asleep faster than those who wrote about
what they had already completed — and the more specific the list, the faster
they fell asleep.[^scullin2018] It is a small study and it wants replication.
But the mechanism is plausible, the intervention costs five minutes, and the
downside is nil.

The general principle is broader than the notebook. The day needs an ending.
Something has to mark the point after which no more problems will be solved
tonight, because without a marker your mind will keep the file open.

## Three: the light has to come down

The circadian system reads light and uses it to decide what time it is. It is
more sensitive than most people expect: under controlled conditions, illuminance
well within the range of ordinary domestic lighting was enough to suppress
melatonin and shift circadian timing.[^zeitzer2000d]

The practical implication is not that you need to sit in the dark. It is that
overhead lighting in the evening is doing something, and that dropping the
household light level for the last hour or two is one of the cheapest changes
available. Lamps rather than ceiling lights. Lower, warmer, and fewer.

We deal with the screen question separately, in [screens before
bed](/wind-down/light-and-screens/screens-before-bed), because the popular
version of that story is considerably more confident than the evidence.

## Two things that quietly undo all of it

A good runway can be cancelled by decisions made much earlier in the day.

**Caffeine has a longer reach than people credit.** Its half-life is around five
to six hours in most adults, which means a late-afternoon coffee is still
substantially present at bedtime. In a controlled study, four hundred milligrams
of caffeine — roughly two strong coffees — measurably disrupted sleep even when
taken six hours before bed, and participants did not reliably notice that it
had.[^drake2013] That last detail is the important one. The absence of a
subjective effect is not evidence of no effect.

If you are sleeping badly and unwilling to give up coffee, the change with the
best ratio of benefit to sacrifice is not less coffee. It is earlier coffee.

**Alcohol is a sedative, not a sleep aid.** It shortens the time it takes to
fall asleep, which is why the belief persists, and then it degrades the rest of
the night: REM is suppressed early on, sleep becomes more fragmented in the
second half, and the effect on the back half of the night gets worse as the dose
rises.[^ebrahim2013] A nightcap trades the part of sleep you notice for the part
you do not.

## What about melatonin?

It comes up constantly, so it is worth being precise. Melatonin is not a
sedative. It is a timing signal — a chronobiotic — and its main effect is on
_when_ your body expects to sleep rather than on how deeply you sleep.

Meta-analysis of melatonin for primary sleep disorders finds a real but modest
effect: people fall asleep somewhat faster and sleep somewhat longer, with
effect sizes smaller than those of prescription hypnotics.[^ferracioli2013] It
is most useful where the problem is genuinely one of timing, such as jet lag or
a delayed sleep phase, and least useful where the problem is arousal at bedtime.

Doses sold over the counter are frequently far higher than anything used in
the timing research, and in several markets the actual content of commercial
products has been found to vary substantially from the label. If you are
considering it, that is a conversation to have with a doctor or pharmacist
rather than with a shelf.

## Putting it together

A ninety-minute runway is not a ritual. It is the minimum time in which the
temperature cascade can get going, arousal can come down, and the light can
drop. Roughly:

**Ninety minutes out.** Last hard exercise done. Bath or warm shower if you are
using one. The day's remaining problems written down and closed.

**Sixty minutes out.** Overhead lights off, lamps on. Work finished — genuinely
finished, not paused. If you are going to look at a screen, this is the point to
decide what for and for how long.

**Thirty minutes out.** Bedroom cool. Nothing demanding. Reading, a bath's
aftermath, quiet conversation, slow breathing if you use it.

**Bed.** No clock in view.

## Where this does not apply

If you fall asleep quickly and sleep through, you do not need a runway and
building one is unlikely to improve anything.

If you have been sleeping badly at least three nights a week for three months or
more, wind-down alone is not the right treatment. The first-line treatment for
chronic insomnia is cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia, which is more
effective than sleep hygiene advice and more effective in the long run than
medication. Ask a doctor about access to it.
