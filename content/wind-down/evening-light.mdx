Here is the number that reframes the problem. Ordinary indoor room lighting —
the kind you would describe as normal, not bright — is enough to suppress
melatonin and shorten the window over which it is released.[^gooley2011] The
circadian pacemaker responds to light across a range that starts far below what
feels bright to you.[^zeitzer2000f]

Your eyes are extremely good at adapting, which is precisely the problem. A room
does not feel bright. It feels like a room.

## Rough magnitudes

A bright overcast day outdoors is on the order of ten thousand lux. A
well-lit office might be five hundred. A domestic living room in the evening is
often a hundred to two hundred. A single warm lamp at the side of the room might
be twenty or thirty. Moonlight is a fraction of one.

The gap between your evening living room and genuine darkness is much larger
than the gap between your living room and your phone.

## What to change

**Stop using the ceiling.** Overhead lighting puts light directly into the upper
field of your vision, which is the part the circadian system attends to most.
Lamps at or below eye level, pointed at walls, do the same job for reading with
a fraction of the effect.

**Fewer of them, lower down, warmer.** If your bulbs are dimmable, use it. If
they are not, the cheapest upgrade in this article is one warm, dim lamp in the
room you spend the evening in.

**Get the bathroom.** People do everything right for two hours and then stand
under a bright bathroom light for ten minutes immediately before bed. A low-
wattage bulb or a separate small light solves it.

**And do the opposite in the morning.** The same sensitivity that works against
you at eleven at night works for you at seven in the morning. Outdoor light
soon after waking is the strongest available signal for anchoring your clock,
and no indoor lamp is a substitute.
