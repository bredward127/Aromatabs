We should start with a caveat that the sleep-hygiene industry rarely offers.
Sleep hygiene advice, delivered on its own as a list of rules, has a weak
evidence base as a treatment for insomnia — it performs poorly enough that it is
often used as the control condition in trials of something else.[^stepanski2003]

That does not make an evening routine useless. It means the routine is
scaffolding for the physiology described in [the ninety-minute
runway](/wind-down/the-runway/the-ninety-minute-runway), not a virtue in itself.
Build it accordingly.

## Why routines fail

**Too long.** A ninety-minute protocol survives a good week and dies in a bad
one. Design the version you can do at eleven at night after a difficult day,
then treat anything more as a bonus.

**Too many new things at once.** Five simultaneous changes give you no idea
which one is doing the work, and five chances to fail.

**Dependent on the environment being right.** If your routine requires a bath,
you have no routine in a hotel or at your parents' house.

**Framed as discipline.** The moment it becomes something you are good or bad
at, a missed night stops being a missed night and becomes evidence.

## A version that survives

Pick one anchor — a single action that reliably marks the end of the day. Making
the same tea. Writing tomorrow's list. Turning off the overhead lights. It needs
to be small, portable and unambiguous.

Attach one physiological lever to it: light down, or temperature. Not both to
begin with.

Give it two weeks before you judge it, and judge it on how often you did it, not
on how you slept. Sleep is too noisy night to night to serve as feedback on a
habit this size.

Then, if it has stuck, add the next thing.
