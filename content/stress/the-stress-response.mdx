There is a version of the stress story that has been repeated so often it has
stopped being examined. It goes: stress is bad, cortisol is the stress hormone,
cortisol is therefore bad, and the goal is less of it.

Almost every step of that is wrong, and the version that replaces it is more
useful.

## What the response actually is

Something happens that your brain classifies as a threat or a demand. Two
systems respond, on different timescales.

The first is fast. Within a second or two, sympathetic outflow rises: heart rate
and blood pressure go up, blood is redistributed toward muscle, glucose is
mobilised, attention narrows. Adrenaline is the familiar chemical here. This
system is built for speed and it discharges quickly.

The second is slower. The hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis — the HPA axis —
runs a hormonal cascade ending in cortisol release from the adrenal glands,
peaking some fifteen to thirty minutes in. Cortisol's job is largely metabolic:
sustaining the energy supply the fast system has already started
spending.[^ulrichlai2009]

Crucially, the HPA axis is a negative feedback loop. Cortisol acts back on the
brain to shut the cascade down. The response is designed to terminate itself.

## Cortisol is not the villain

Cortisol is essential. Remove it entirely and you die. It follows a pronounced
daily rhythm — a sharp rise in the half-hour after waking, then a long decline
across the day — and that rhythm is part of what gets you out of bed.

Sapolsky and colleagues made the argument that glucocorticoids often act to
_contain_ the stress response rather than to mediate it: preventing the body's
own defensive reactions from overshooting.[^sapolsky2000] On that account
cortisol is less an alarm than a brake.

What the research associates with harm is not cortisol as such. It is prolonged
elevation, blunted daily rhythms, and a system that no longer switches off
cleanly.

## The part that matters: it should have ended by now

Here is the finding that reframes everything.

A stress response to an actual event is brief. The event resolves, the feedback
loop closes, and physiology returns to baseline in minutes to hours. That
sequence appears to be well tolerated.

But most human stress responses are not triggered by events in front of us. They
are triggered by _thinking about_ events — anticipating them, rehearsing them,
replaying them. Brosschot, Gerin and Thayer named this perseverative cognition,
and their argument is that worry and rumination extend the physiological stress
response far beyond the stressor itself.[^brosschot2006]

This is the mechanism by which a difficult conversation at eleven in the morning
is still raising your heart rate at eleven at night. The conversation lasted
twenty minutes. The response has lasted twelve hours, because you have kept
re-presenting the stimulus to yourself.

It also explains why "reduce your stress" is such unhelpful advice. The
stressors are often not removable. The perseveration sometimes is.

## What chronic activation costs

Bruce McEwen's framework for this is allostatic load: the cumulative wear that
accrues when the systems that adapt you to demand are chronically engaged, or
fail to switch off, or fail to switch on when needed.[^mcewen1998]

The consequences documented across that literature include cardiovascular
effects, metabolic effects, immune effects, and effects on the hippocampus and
prefrontal cortex — the very regions involved in regulating the response in the
first place, which is one of the more unpleasant feedback loops in physiology.

We treat this at more length in
[allostatic load](/stress/the-stress-response/allostatic-load).

## Where sleep comes in

Psychosocial stress measurably degrades sleep. Reviews of polysomnographic
studies find longer sleep onset, more fragmentation, and reductions in slow-wave
sleep under stress.[^kim2007]

And the relationship runs both ways. Short sleep raises reactivity to the
following day's demands, which produces more stress, which produces worse sleep.
For a great many people, the sleep problem and the stress problem are one
problem with two names — which is why our [wind-down](/wind-down) material and
this pillar keep pointing at each other.

## So what helps

Not "less stress", which is usually not on offer.

**Close the loop deliberately.** The response ends when the brain accepts the
episode is over. Physical activity, a completed conversation, writing the thing
down — these are all ways of supplying an ending. See
[worry time](/stress/recovery/worry-time) for a structured version.

**Interrupt the perseveration rather than the stressor.** You often cannot
change the event. You can change how many times you re-run it.

**Use a lever that acts fast.** [Slow breathing](/breath) is the most direct
voluntary access anyone has to the autonomic side of this, and it works within a
few breaths.

**Protect sleep first when both are bad.** It is the variable with the most
downstream reach.

## When to see a doctor

Persistent low mood, anxiety that interferes with daily life, panic attacks, or
stress you cannot get relief from are matters for a clinician, not for
self-management. So are unexplained physical symptoms. Nothing in this article
is a treatment, and stress-related illness is treatable.
