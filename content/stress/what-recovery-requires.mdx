You can spend an entire evening doing nothing and arrive at bedtime as wound up
as you were at six. This is a common experience and it is not a failure of
willpower. It is the difference between stopping and detaching.

## Detachment is the active ingredient

The occupational health literature on recovery converges on psychological
detachment — mentally disengaging from work during non-work time — as the
component most consistently associated with better outcomes.[^sonnentag2015]
Not the amount of free time. Not what you do with it. Whether you were
actually elsewhere.

That fits the physiology directly: if perseverative cognition is what extends
the stress response, then time spent not perseverating is what ends
it.[^brosschot2006b] An evening on the sofa rehearsing tomorrow's meeting is,
physiologically, more of the meeting.

## What tends to produce detachment

**Absorption.** Activities that occupy enough attention to crowd out
rehearsal. This is why people report that gardening, cooking, playing an
instrument or physical exercise work better than passively watching something,
which leaves plenty of cognitive room free.

**Boundaries that are real.** Detachment collapses if the work is one
notification away. The evidence here is thinner than the headlines suggest, but
the mechanism is straightforward.

**An ending.** A deliberate marker that the working day is over. The commute
used to supply this for a lot of people and now often does not.

## What does not

Doing nothing, alone, with a phone. Alcohol, which suppresses arousal without
producing detachment and then costs you the second half of the night. And
"relaxing" activities chosen because they are supposed to be relaxing rather
than because they occupy you.

## The honest caveat

Most of this literature is observational and self-reported, and detachment is
measured by questionnaire. The relationships are consistent and plausible; the
causal evidence is thinner than anyone would like. Treat it as a useful frame
rather than an established mechanism.
