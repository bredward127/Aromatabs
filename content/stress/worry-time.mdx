Thought suppression is unreliable and often counterproductive. Told not to think
about something, most people think about it more.

Worry postponement takes the opposite approach. You do not try to stop worrying.
You agree with yourself to do it later, at a specific time, in a specific place.

## The method

1. **Pick a slot.** Fifteen to thirty minutes, the same time each day, not
   within two hours of bed. Early evening works for most people.
2. **Notice worries as they arrive.** Write down a few words — enough to find
   the thought again — and return to what you were doing.
3. **Keep the appointment.** Sit down at the slot and go through the list
   deliberately. Where something is actionable, write the next action. Where it
   is not, note that.
4. **Stop when the time is up.**

## Why it works better than it sounds

The technique comes from Borkovec's stimulus-control work on worry, which
treated worry as a behaviour under the control of cues — occurring in many
settings, at all hours — and set out to restrict it to a single time and
place.[^borkovec1983]

The postponement itself does most of the work. Deferring is a much easier
instruction to follow than stopping, because you are not being asked to give the
thought up. And the promise is credible only if you actually keep the
appointment, which is the step people skip and the reason it then fails.

The bedtime-writing evidence points the same way: writing a specific to-do list
before bed produced faster sleep onset than writing about completed
tasks.[^scullin2018b] Getting the open loop out of your head and somewhere
retrievable appears to be the mechanism.

## What it is not

It is not a treatment for generalised anxiety disorder, although it is a
component of some therapies for it. If worry is persistent, uncontrollable and
interfering with your life, that is worth taking to a doctor — and it responds
well to treatment.
