Of everything in your bedroom, temperature has the best-established effect on
sleep. It is also the variable people get backwards most often, because the
folk model — warm is cosy, cosy is sleepy — describes how a warm room feels
rather than what it does.

## The mechanism

Your core body temperature follows a daily rhythm. It peaks in the early
evening and falls through the night, reaching its lowest point a couple of hours
before you normally wake. That fall is not incidental to sleep; it is part of
how sleep begins.

To lose core heat you have to move it to the surface and let it go. In the
evening the blood vessels in your hands and feet dilate, heat flows outward, and
your core temperature drops. The size of that gradient — warm extremities,
cooler core — turns out to be a strong predictor of how quickly you fall
asleep, better in one influential study than melatonin levels or how sleepy
people said they felt.[^krauchi1999b]

A cool room does not make you sleepy directly. It gives the heat somewhere to
go.

## What too warm does

The research on thermal environment and sleep is unusually consistent. Heat
exposure increases wakefulness and reduces both slow-wave sleep and REM. It does
so more when humidity is high, because sweating is the fallback mechanism and
humid air makes evaporation less effective.[^okamoto2012]

Cold has effects too, but they are asymmetric. Moderate cold is much better
tolerated than moderate heat, largely because bedding lets you adjust. This is
why the practical advice is a cool room and adequate covers rather than a warm
room — you can add a blanket, but you cannot easily shed core heat in a stuffy
bedroom at two in the morning.

## The warm bath, correctly explained

A warm bath or shower before bed improves sleep, and it does so by cooling you
down.

Passive body heating dilates peripheral blood vessels. When you get out, that
dilation persists and your core sheds heat faster than it otherwise would. The
meta-analysis found improvements in sleep onset latency, sleep efficiency and
subjective quality, with the effect largest when the bath came roughly one to
two hours before bed.[^haghayegh2019b]

The timing is the part usually omitted. A bath ten minutes before bed does not
have time to work through the mechanism.

## Numbers, with a caveat

Most guidance lands somewhere between sixteen and nineteen degrees Celsius. That
range is a reasonable starting point, and it is also less meaningful than it
looks, because air temperature is only one input. Bedding, clothing, mattress
material, humidity and what your partner needs all change the effective thermal
environment, and the studies that produce these numbers use controlled bedding
that you do not have.

Treat the room temperature as one of several dials rather than the answer.

## What to actually do

**Get the room cooler than feels intuitive.** Most bedrooms are warmer than they
should be, particularly in winter with heating on a timer.

**Fix the bedding before the thermostat.** A duvet rated for deep winter used
year-round undoes any amount of thermostat management. A lighter tog, or two
separate duvets if you and a partner run at different temperatures, is often the
single most effective change.

**Warm your extremities.** Socks in bed sound unglamorous and are well supported
by the mechanism: warm feet accelerate the heat loss that gets you to sleep.
This is not in tension with a cool room. It is the other half of it.

**Time the bath.** One to two hours out, not immediately before.

**Deal with humidity as well as heat.** A warm, humid room is considerably worse
than a warm, dry one. See
[air and humidity](/environment/temperature-and-air/air-and-humidity).

**Consider the early morning separately.** Core temperature bottoms out a couple
of hours before waking, and a room that was comfortable at midnight can be too
cold at five. If you wake reliably at a similar early hour, a small amount of
heating timed for then is worth trying before anything more elaborate.

## When it is not the room

Night sweats that soak bedding, waking hot with a racing heart, or a change in
temperature tolerance that arrived without an obvious cause are worth raising
with a doctor rather than solving with a thinner duvet.
