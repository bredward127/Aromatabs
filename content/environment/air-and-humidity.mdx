A bedroom with the door and window shut accumulates carbon dioxide overnight,
often several times outdoor levels by morning. Whether that matters has been
tested directly.

In a study that manipulated bedroom ventilation while keeping temperature
controlled, better ventilation was associated with improved sleep quality and
better next-day performance on a test of logical thinking.[^strom2016] The
effects were real but modest, and the sample was small.

That is roughly where the evidence sits: a plausible mechanism, a small number
of well-designed studies pointing the same way, nothing that would justify
buying equipment.

## Humidity matters mostly through temperature

Humidity's clearest effect on sleep is that it interferes with heat loss. Warm
and humid is substantially worse than warm and dry, because evaporation is your
fallback cooling mechanism and humid air blunts it.[^okamoto2012b]

Very dry air has its own costs — dry throat, irritated sinuses, more disturbed
sleep if you already have a respiratory issue — but the effects are on comfort
more than on sleep architecture. Somewhere between forty and sixty percent
relative humidity is the usual comfort recommendation, and it is a comfort
recommendation rather than a sleep one.

## What we would suggest

Open a window, or leave the door ajar. That is the whole intervention, and it
costs nothing.

If you cannot — noise, pollution, security — then a cracked window earlier in
the evening, or ventilating the room thoroughly before bed, gets you part of the
way.

We would not buy an air purifier for sleep on this evidence. If you have
allergies, that is a different question with a different literature.
