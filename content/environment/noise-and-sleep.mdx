The systematic review underpinning the WHO's environmental noise guidelines is
unusually clear on one point: night-time noise causes measurable sleep
disturbance — arousals, awakenings, changes in sleep stage and increases in
motility — at levels well below those that people report being woken
by.[^basner2018]

In other words, you can be disturbed by traffic all night and remember an
uninterrupted night's sleep.

## What matters is change, not volume

A constant sound is much less disruptive than an intermittent one at the same
level. Your auditory system continues monitoring during sleep and responds to
novelty. A steady hum is quickly discounted. A door closing at three in the
morning is not.

This is the whole basis of masking. Broadband noise does not make your room
quieter; it raises the floor so that the _changes_ stand out less.

## Does white noise work?

Less clearly than the market for it suggests. A systematic review of noise as a
sleep aid found the evidence weak and inconsistent, with small studies, varied
methods, and a real possibility that continuous noise exposure carries its own
costs.[^riedy2021] The authors were explicit that they could not recommend it on
current evidence.

Our reading: if masking helps you, that is a legitimate reason to use it. It is
not something we would suggest adding on the strength of the literature, and we
would keep the volume as low as does the job.

## The order we would try things in

**Remove the source** where you can. A phone on silent, in another room.

**Block it** next. Well-fitted earplugs are cheap, effective and much better
supported than any electronic solution. Foam ones need to be inserted properly
to do anything; most people under-insert them.

**Mask it** last, and quietly.

If a partner's snoring is what is waking you, earplugs are a workaround for you
and not a solution for them. Loud habitual snoring, especially with witnessed
pauses in breathing, is worth a doctor's assessment.
