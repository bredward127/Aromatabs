Almost everything your autonomic nervous system does is closed to you. You
cannot decide to lower your blood pressure, or instruct your gut to slow down,
or choose to sweat less.

Breathing is the exception. It runs automatically, and it also accepts
deliberate input. That makes it the one place where conscious control reaches
into a system that is otherwise sealed — which is why it turns up in every
contemplative tradition anyone has written down, and why it has become the most
studied self-regulation technique in the physiological literature.

The mechanism is real and reasonably well understood. The claims made for it in
popular coverage run considerably ahead of it. Both of those things are worth
setting out carefully.

## Your heart rate is already following your breath

Put your fingers on your pulse and breathe slowly. Your heart speeds up slightly
as you inhale and slows as you exhale.

This is respiratory sinus arrhythmia, and it is normal — a healthy sign, in
fact, not a fault. During inhalation, vagal outflow to the heart is briefly
withdrawn and the rate rises. During exhalation, vagal influence returns and the
rate falls. The vagus nerve is the main parasympathetic pathway to the heart,
and it acts fast: unlike sympathetic signalling, which takes seconds to build,
vagal effects appear within a single heartbeat.

That speed is what makes breathing a usable lever. A longer exhale means a
longer stretch of vagal influence. Slow your breathing and you extend those
stretches, which is why deliberately lengthening the exhale lowers heart rate
within a few breaths.[^russo2017]

## The resonance frequency

Here is where it gets more specific, and more interesting.

Your cardiovascular system contains a feedback loop — the baroreflex — that
regulates blood pressure. Sensors in the arteries detect pressure changes and
the system adjusts heart rate to compensate. Like any feedback loop with a
delay, it has a frequency at which it resonates.

For most adults that frequency corresponds to a breathing rate of roughly
four-and-a-half to seven breaths per minute, with around six being a common
centre. Breathe at that rate and the oscillations in your heart rate driven by
breathing line up with those driven by the baroreflex. The two reinforce each
other, and the amplitude of heart rate variability increases sharply — much more
than at breathing rates slightly above or below.[^vaschillo2006][^lehrer2014]

This is not mysticism, and it is not a metaphor. It is a resonance effect in a
control system, and it is measurable within a couple of minutes. It is also why
so many breathing protocols converge on a rate near six per minute despite
having arrived there from completely different traditions.

## What actually happens when you do it

A systematic review of slow breathing in healthy adults found a fairly
consistent picture: increased heart rate variability, shifts in the balance of
autonomic activity toward parasympathetic dominance, changes in EEG that are
broadly consistent with reduced arousal, and reports of greater comfort,
relaxation and alertness.[^zaccaro2018]

A meta-analysis focused specifically on the heart rate variability outcomes
confirmed that voluntary slow breathing reliably increases the measures most
associated with vagal activity.[^laborde2022]

More recently, a randomised trial compared three five-minute daily breathing
practices against mindfulness meditation over a month. All four improved mood
and reduced respiratory rate; the breathing practices did somewhat better than
meditation, and the best performer was cyclic sighing — a double inhale through
the nose followed by an extended exhale.[^balban2023] It is a single trial with
a young, largely self-selected sample, and it should not be treated as settled.
It is, though, a well-designed piece of work pointing the same way as the
mechanism.

## What the evidence does not show

This is the part usually left out.

**Most measured effects are short-lived.** The autonomic changes during and
shortly after a session are robust. Evidence that regular practice produces
lasting change in resting physiology is much thinner, and the studies are
generally small, short, and hard to blind — you cannot give someone a placebo
breathing exercise.

**Effects on clinical outcomes are far less certain than effects on
physiology.** Heart rate variability going up is not the same as anxiety coming
down, and it is certainly not the same as a condition being treated. Trials in
clinical populations exist and some are encouraging, but the literature is
heterogeneous and the risk of publication bias in a field this easy to run small
studies in is not trivial.

**"Activating the vagus nerve" is a phrase to be suspicious of.** Slow breathing
increases vagal influence on the heart. That is a specific, measurable claim.
The popular version — that you can toggle your nervous system into a restorative
state and keep it there — is not something the research supports.

## What to take from it

If you want a single practical conclusion: slowing your breathing to around six
per minute, with the exhale longer than the inhale, will reliably lower your
heart rate and shift your autonomic balance while you are doing it, and will
generally make you feel calmer. That is a real effect with a real mechanism,
available at no cost, with essentially no downside.

Whether it changes anything durable is a question the evidence has not yet
answered, and anyone who tells you otherwise is ahead of the data.

The specific patterns — 4-7-8, box breathing, coherent breathing — are covered

## A caution

Slow breathing is safe for most people. It is not appropriate as a treatment for
a diagnosed anxiety disorder, and it is not a substitute for asthma medication.
Stop if you become dizzy or light-headed, which usually means you are breathing
more air rather than less. Breath-holding practices and deliberate
hyperventilation carry real risks and are outside what we cover — never do them
in or near water.
